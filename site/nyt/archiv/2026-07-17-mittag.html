U.S. Strikes Iranian Targets as Shipping and Oil Markets Convulse

What's new: The U.S. military's Central Command struck dozens of Iranian military targets in the country's south, including bridges and a port facility. The strikes mark an escalation in the Iran war that is now materially disrupting global energy markets: shipping traffic in the Persian Gulf has fallen to its lowest level in more than a month, and oil prices have risen in response. The disruption threatens a critical chokepoint for global energy supply.

Why it matters: The Strait of Hormuz carries roughly a third of the world's seaborne oil. As conflict intensifies, even temporary supply interruptions can ripple through U.S. energy prices and broader inflation—a factor the Federal Reserve watches closely as it considers future rate moves. For you as a consumer, sustained shipping disruptions could mean higher gas and heating costs this winter.

What's next: Markets will likely remain volatile as long as military operations continue. Any further escalation or damage to port infrastructure could trigger sharper price spikes. (Business · By: Jenny Gross). Read in the NYT →

Trump's Election-Tampering Claims Lack Supporting Evidence

What's new: President Trump released documentary evidence he promised would prove foreign election meddling, but the materials fell significantly short of bombshell revelations. Chinese officials rejected the meddling accusations as baseless while signaling they want to preserve diplomatic stability.

Why it matters: The weak evidentiary showing undermines Trump's central claim about the election and potentially weakens the political case for his related policy moves. It also signals that Beijing is not looking for an escalatory response—a rare point of restraint amid broader U.S.-China tensions. (Politics · By: David E. Sanger and Dustin Volz). Read in the NYT →

North Carolina Republicans Restrict Early Voting

What's new: Republican lawmakers in North Carolina have moved to reduce early voting opportunities on Sundays and on college campuses ahead of the 2026 midterms. Voting-rights advocates say the changes are designed to suppress turnout among Black voters and students—demographics that tend to vote Democratic.

Why it matters: With the Voting Rights Act significantly weakened, states have more latitude to reshape voting access. These changes signal a broader Republican strategy to alter electoral mechanics in contested states, which could shift the political math in close races. (US · By: Nick Corasaniti). Fed May Raise Rates Despite Cooling Inflation

The Federal Reserve is expected to hold rates steady at its July meeting, but officials have signaled they have not ruled out increases in the near term—even as inflation data has improved. This suggests the Fed may prioritize other concerns, such as labor-market strength, over recent disinflationary trends. (Business · By: Colby Smith). Read in the NYT →

France Adapts Firefighting as Blazes Move North

Forest fires are now spreading into regions north of Paris—a shift driven by extreme heat that is forcing France to overhaul its firefighting strategy, which was designed for southern blazes. The northward spread signals how climate patterns are reshaping fire risk across Europe. (World · By: Catherine Porter and Dmitry Kostyukov). Read in the NYT →
