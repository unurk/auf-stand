U.S. and Iran Trade Military Strikes; Oil Prices Jump

What's new: The ceasefire between the United States and Iran is unraveling. Over the weekend, the U.S. military struck dozens of Iranian targets, and Iran's army responded by firing drones at American military sites in the region. The escalation centers on the Strait of Hormuz, a waterway critical to global energy supplies. Oil prices have surged as a result, reflecting traders' concern that the renewed hostilities could disrupt shipping through one of the world's most important chokepoints.

Why it matters: This represents a significant breakdown of an agreement President Trump had signed with Iran that the administration claimed would secure American interests in the region. The volatility now threatens not just the region's stability but also your energy costs and broader economic growth. The pattern suggests Trump's original deal—which Iran interpreted as giving it control of the strait—has collapsed into the kind of military confrontation he sought to avoid.

What's next: Both sides are positioned for further escalation. Hard-liners within Iran's military have moved to fill leadership gaps created by earlier casualties and are pushing to intensify the fight.

Senator Lindsey Graham Dies; Ukraine Loses a Key Champion

What's new: Senator Lindsey Graham of South Carolina died Saturday night from an aortic dissection, a tear in the body's main artery. He was 79 and had just returned from Ukraine days before. Graham's death triggers an August 11 special Republican primary, and South Carolina's governor will appoint a replacement to serve through early January.

Why it matters: Graham was Ukraine's most influential advocate within President Trump's inner circle—a significant loss at a moment when the war remains unresolved and Ukraine needs American support. His death also underscores a broader vulnerability in Congress: the Senate's average age exceeds 65, concentrating critical foreign policy decisions in the hands of aging lawmakers.

Japan Establishes New Intelligence Agency to Counter Russia and China

What's new: Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi is building a new national intelligence agency with assistance from Western allies, marking a significant break with Japan's post-World War II security restraints. The move reflects mounting concerns about Russian and Chinese threats, including evidence that Russia has been operating an extensive espionage network from Tokyo to obtain high-tech components for its war effort in Ukraine.

Why it matters: Japan's pivot toward enhanced intelligence capabilities signals a broader shift in Asian geopolitics, as the region braces for intensifying great-power competition. For the U.S., it represents a strengthening of alliance structures in the Indo-Pacific at a time when stability there is increasingly fragile.

State attorneys general are preparing to file suit as soon as this week to block the $111 billion deal, which would consolidate significant media and entertainment power and poses a major legal hurdle to the merger's completion.
